# Performance-first footage selection

Read this reference for scripted scenes with alternate takes, generated coverage, conflicting eyelines, or continuity defects. Its purpose is to prevent technically clean footage from displacing the stronger dramatic choice.

## Unit of selection

Select the smallest useful emotional or informational moment, not the whole source clip. A take that fails overall may still contain a strong glance, breath, action phase, or reaction lasting 0.5–3 seconds. Record that salvageable range separately from the rest of the take.

Before ranking footage, define the beat:

- the character objective and current holder of power;
- the new information or emotional consequence;
- what the audience should notice at this instant;
- what the next image must change.

For dialogue, build or verify the production-sound spine first. Picture may show the speaker, listener, relationship, evidence, or action while the selected dialogue continues off screen. A verified word boundary protects audio correctness; it does not require retaining the corresponding picture.

## Candidate coverage

For each important beat, look for candidates in five functions:

1. speaker performance;
2. listener or affected-character reaction;
3. two-shot or relationship geometry;
4. action, prop, evidence, or environment insert;
5. neutral bridge that can reset direction or conceal a discontinuity.

Do not decorate a scene with reaction shots. Use one when it reveals consequence, changes power, delays or releases information, or repairs a necessary edit.

## Lexicographic ranking

Rank candidates in this order unless the scene establishes a justified exception:

1. truthful performance and dramatic turn;
2. required story information and audience attention;
3. action phase, timing, and editability;
4. eyeline, axis, prop, wardrobe, and spatial continuity;
5. technical cleanliness.

This is not permission to ignore defects. It prevents a lower-level geometric preference from replacing a materially better performance before repair options are considered.

Keep artistic value and defect risk separate in the selection record. Use this decision matrix:

| Dramatic value | Defect risk | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| strong | low or low-salience | use directly |
| strong | visible but coverable | preserve the performance or audio; cover the defect |
| strong | wrong screen direction | try a bridge, insert, action cut, reaction, then a low-risk single-shot mirror |
| weak | technically clean | reserve for orientation, bridging, or necessary information |
| weak | visible defect | reject |

## Defect salience and repairability

Judge a defect by first-viewer salience, duration, narrative importance, and repair cost rather than by its mere presence.

Usually reject or cover:

- identity or face changes;
- severe anatomy or physics failure;
- action that reverses or visibly restarts;
- incorrect story-critical text or object state;
- a persistent error near the audience's attention target.

Potentially acceptable when the dramatic gain is clear:

- brief background or bedding changes;
- lighting variation hidden by a motivated cut;
- non-story wardrobe detail in a short reaction;
- low-salience signage or prop inconsistency during a strong emotional turn.

Record the compromise. Do not rely on the claim that viewers will not notice unless the preview supports it.

## Continuity repair order

When the best performance conflicts with continuity, attempt repairs in this order:

1. neutral or re-establishing shot;
2. motivated insert;
3. cut on a matching action phase;
4. listener reaction or off-screen dialogue;
5. low-risk mirror of a single shot;
6. alternate take.

Mirror only when it materially improves eyeline or screen direction and no less invasive repair preserves the performance. Avoid mirroring two-person physical action. Before approving a mirror, inspect text, logos, watches, jewelry, garment closures, injuries, props, handedness, lighting direction, and recognizable geography. Recheck the transformed range at 0.2 seconds or denser.

Reframe or crop only when it improves attention, removes a repairable edge defect, or makes coverage compatible. Do not enlarge merely to simulate variety. Inspect resolution, skin, hands, headroom, motion, and composition after the transform.

## Compression and omission

Preserve an action only when it establishes geography, changes power, delivers information, creates suspense, or pays off emotion. Walking, door access, phone handling, sitting, and other procedural phases may be omitted once the audience can infer them.

For every proposed shot ask:

- Why does the cut occur now?
- What does this image add or change?
- Is the pause expressive or inherited dead time?
- Can the same value survive in a shorter range?
- Does a technical compromise buy a visible dramatic improvement?

If none has a concrete answer, remove the shot or shorten it.

## Preview comparison

When selection remains ambiguous, render short alternatives rather than debating isolated frames:

- a performance-first version that uses the strongest moments and documented repairs;
- a continuity-first version that uses cleaner geometry.

Compare without music first. Judge intention, power, emotional consequence, information timing, first-viewer defect salience, and whether every shot earns its duration. Prefer the version with the stronger scene unless its remaining defect is story-breaking or repeatedly distracting.

Persist the chosen candidate, rejected alternatives, dramatic reason, defect class, salience, repair, and preview result in `selection_matrix.md` or equivalent project memory.
